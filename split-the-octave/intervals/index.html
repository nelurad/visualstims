Intervalsthe space between two notes

Every tune is a string of little jumps, and every chord is a stack of them. The size of the jump decides whether two notes hug or bicker. Turn your volume on.

You should hear a few soft notes. Nothing? Turn your volume up. On an iPhone, also flip off the silent switch.
1

An interval is a distance

Pick any two notes. The gap between them is called an interval. That's all it is: a distance, like the space between two stepping stones.

You already own the ruler. A half step is the move from one key to the very next key, black or white (lesson 4). To measure an interval, count the half steps from one note to the other.

TRY IT · tap one key, then another. Watch the hops get counted.

Musicians give the common distances names, like third or fifth. Those names are old and a little odd (they count letter names, not keys), so don't sweat them. The half-step count is the real measurement, and you'll pick up the names by ear.

The big idea: an interval is the distance between two notes, measured in half steps.
2

One after the other, or both at once

You can hear an interval two ways. Play the notes one after the other and you get a tiny tune: a melodic interval. Play them at the same time and they melt into one sound: a harmonic interval.

Same distance, different experience. A melody is the leap your voice makes when you sing. Harmony is the colour you get when two people sing different notes together.

TRY IT · flip the switch and pick a distance. Each tap plays it.

The big idea: melody is intervals in a row; harmony is intervals stacked up.
3

Meet the family

Inside one octave there are twelve distances, plus zero (the same note twice). No need to memorise them today. Tap around and notice that each one has its own personality.

The trick musicians use to remember them is a song hook: the first two notes of a well-known tune hand you the interval for free. Hum along.

TRY IT · tap a card. You'll hear the hook's two notes, then both together.

The big idea: each interval has its own flavour, and a familiar song can be its name tag.
4

Sweet or sour?

Some pairs sound smooth together. Others grind like a squeaky gate. Musicians call smooth pairs consonant and tense ones dissonant. Neither is wrong; sour is a flavour too.

Why the difference? Every note is a wobble in the air (lesson 1). When two notes sound together, their wobbles sometimes line up. If they line up often, in a simple pattern like 2 wobbles against 3, the mix sounds calm. If they almost never line up, like 16 against 15, the mix shudders.

5

Tension wants to go home

A sour interval isn't a mistake. It's a question. Music uses tension the way a story uses a cliffhanger: it makes you lean in and wait. Moving from a tense interval to a calm one is called resolving.

6

Same shape, anywhere

Here's a lovely thing: an interval keeps its flavour wherever you put it. A fifth starting on C sounds like a fifth starting on F, just higher. Moving music up or down while keeping its shape is called transposing.

That's why you can start Happy Birthday on any note and it's still Happy Birthday. The distances stay the same; only the starting point moves.

TRY IT · choose a distance, then tap any key. The same shape gets built on top of it.

The big idea: an interval is a shape, not a place: move it anywhere and it keeps its flavour.
7

Train your ear

Start with three very different distances: a whole step (Happy Birthday's little step), a fifth (Twinkle's jump) and an octave (Some-where over the rainbow). Each question plays the two notes one after the other, then together. Think of the hooks.

Get three in a row and the next level opens up.
