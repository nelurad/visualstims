Between the letters

Last time you met the white keys and their letter names, A B C D E F G, repeating up the keyboard. But look again: between most of the white keys sits a skinny black key. Those are notes too, with sounds of their own.

So how many different notes are there before the pattern repeats? Let’s count. Start on C and play every white key up to the next C: that’s 7. Now play every key, black and white: that’s 12.

TRY IT · press a button and count along. The circle lights up as each note plays.

7 white + 5 black = 12 different notes. Then C comes round again, one octave up.

Twelve notes, over and over, from the lowest key to the highest. That’s every note a piano has; it just repeats them in higher and lower versions. (Why exactly twelve? That’s a story for lesson 9.)

The big idea: one octave holds 12 different notes: 7 white keys plus 5 black keys.
2

The half step

Going from one key to the very next key, with nothing in between, is the smallest step in our music. It’s called a half step. (Some people call it a semitone: same thing, fancier word.)

The next key might be black or white. It doesn’t matter: if you can’t squeeze another key in between, it’s a half step. Meet the inchworm. It can only move one key at a time.

TRY IT · press the arrows (or ← → on a keyboard) to crawl one half step at a time. Tap any key to drop the worm there.

Hear that creeping, sneaky sound when the worm crawls the whole way? Every key, one after the other. Musicians call that a chromatic run (from the Greek for “colour”), and film composers love it for tiptoeing cartoon villains.

The big idea: a half step is the move from one key to the very next key, black or white.
3

Sharp ♯ and flat ♭

So what are the black keys called? They borrow the names of their white neighbours, plus a little sign.

sharp ♯one half step up
flat ♭one half step down

A sharp (♯, like a little hashtag) means one half step up. A flat (♭, like a squashed lowercase b) means one half step down. So the black key just to the right of C is C♯, said “C sharp”. The black key just to the left of B is B♭, said “B flat”.

TRY IT · pick a white key, then press ♯ or ♭. Watch where it lands, and listen to the step.

The big idea: ♯ (sharp) means one half step up and ♭ (flat) means one half step down.
4

One key, two names

Here’s the twist. The black key between C and D is one half step above C, so it’s C♯. But it’s also one half step below D, so it’s D♭. Which is it? Both.

Think of a friend called Elizabeth. Her teachers say “Elizabeth”, her friends say “Liz”. Same person, two names. Every black key works like that: one sound, two spellings. The grown-up word for this is enharmonic, which just means “sounds the same, written differently”.

TRY IT · tap a name tag to walk there from that neighbour. Tap the key itself to hear it alone.

So why have two names at all? Mostly it’s about direction: walking up a tune, musicians tend to say sharps; walking down, flats. Later you’ll see that a song’s scale decides the spelling. For now, either name is right.

The big idea: every black key has two names, like C♯ and D♭, but they’re the very same key and the very same sound.
5

The sneaky spots

Look closely at the keyboard. Black keys come in a group of two, then a group of three. And in two places, two white keys sit side by side with no black key between them: E and F, and B and C.

No key in between means they’re already neighbours. So E to F is just a half step, and so is B to C. Compare that with C to D, where a black key sits in the gap.

TRY IT · count the steps in each walk. The sneaky pairs are coloured in.

C to D crosses a black key. E to F doesn’t.

AND SO… what is one half step above E?

Yes, E♯ is just F. Sharp still means “one half step up”, and the next key up from E happens to be white. You won’t see E♯ often, but it’s a fun one to remember, and it proves that ♯ and ♭ are about moving, not about being black.

The big idea: E–F and B–C have no black key between them, so they are already a half step apart (and E♯ is F).
6

The whole step

Two half steps glued together make a whole step (or a tone). On the keyboard, it means skip one key and land on the next. C to D is a whole step (skipping C♯). E to F♯ is a whole step too (skipping F).

Musicians shorten these to W for whole and H for half. Now you can walk anywhere using only those two letters.

Did the first recipe sound familiar? Starting on C, W W H W W W H plays “do re mi fa so la ti do”: the major scale. The second one sounds sadder: that’s a minor scale. A scale is really just a pattern of whole and half steps. Lesson 7 is all about that.

The big idea: a whole step is two half steps (skip one key), and patterns of W and H steps build scales.
7

Hearing the difference

One half step sounds tiny. Does it matter? Play a tune you know, then nudge just one note up by a half step. Suddenly it sounds wrong, or at least spicy, like a singer who slipped.

TRY IT · play it right first. Then play the sneaky version and tap the note that went sharp.

Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star. Tap any blob to hear its note.

Your ear is very good at this. Most people can’t name a single note, yet almost everyone hears when one note in a familiar tune is a half step off.

The big idea: a half step is small, but moving one note by a half step can change how a tune feels.
