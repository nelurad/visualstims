Tone colourone note, a hundred voices

A flute and a trumpet can play the exact same note, and you would still never mix them up. This lesson is about that difference: what it is, where it hides, and how your ear spots it in a split second. Turn your volume on.

Same note, nine voices

In lesson 1 you learned that pitch is just how fast something wobbles. So here is a puzzle. Below, nine instruments all play the very same note: a C, wobbling 262 times a second. Same speed, every one. Tap them.

Same pitch. Yet the flute sounds soft and breathy, the trumpet sounds bright and bold, the bell shimmers. That difference has a name: timbre, said “TAM-ber”. It's a fussy word, so most people use a friendlier one: tone colour.

Start the tune, then tap other instruments while it plays. The melody stays exactly the same. Only the colour changes, like repainting a drawing without moving a single line.

look at the wiggly line: every instrument draws its own shape!
The big idea: timbre, or tone colour, is what makes two sounds different even when their pitch and loudness are the same.
2

The hidden ingredients

Here is the secret. When a string or a tube of air vibrates, it doesn't wobble in just one simple way. The whole string swings back and forth, and that gives the note you hear. But at the same time its halves wobble twice as fast, its thirds three times as fast, and so on.

So a real note is a stack: the main wobble at the bottom, with quieter, faster wobbles riding on top. These extras are called harmonics. You don't hear them as separate notes. They melt together into one sound, and the mix is what gives that sound its colour.

recipes

Start with Pure: just the main wobble, alone. That smooth wave sounds like a whistle or a tuning fork: clean, but a bit dull. Now push up more bars and listen to it get brighter and buzzier. The Clarinet recipe uses only the odd harmonics (1, 3, 5, 7), which gives that hollow, woody sound.

Why do they melt into one note?Harmonic 2 wobbles exactly twice as fast as the main wobble, harmonic 3 exactly three times, and so on. Because they all fit neatly inside the main wobble's rhythm, your brain glues them into one sound instead of hearing a chord. (Lesson 9 goes deep on this.)
The big idea: every real note is a stack of harmonics, and the recipe of that stack is a big part of its tone colour.
3

A sound has a shape in time

Harmonics are only half the story. The other half is how a sound starts, lasts and stops. Listen to how each of these begins and ends:

A plucked or struck note jumps in sharp, then fades away all by itself. A bowed or blown note swells up and keeps going for as long as the player keeps bowing or blowing. This outline of loudness over time is called the envelope. It has four parts:

Attackhow fast it gets loud
Decayhow far it drops back right after
Sustainhow loud it stays while held
Releasehow long it fades when let go

Here is the surprising part: these pads always use the same recipe of harmonics. Only the envelope changes. Yet Pluck sounds like a harp, Bow sounds like a soft string section, and Organ sounds like, well, an organ. Hold a pad and notice that a pluck fades even while you keep your finger down, just like a real string.

The first split secondThe very start of a note carries a lot of its identity. In experiments where the first fraction of a second of each note is snipped off, people get much worse at naming the instrument.
The big idea: tone colour is not just the ingredients; it is also the shape of the sound over time, especially how it begins.
4

Two separate knobs

So a sound has (at least) two separate settings. One is how fast it wobbles: that's pitch, from lesson 1. The other is the shape of each wobble: that's tone colour. They don't get in each other's way. You can turn one and leave the other exactly where it is.

TURN THE KNOBS · drag up or down, or focus one and use the arrow keys · the picture shows 1/50 of a second

Count the wiggles. Turning the pitch knob squeezes more wiggles into the same little slice of time, but each one keeps its shape. Turning the colour knob changes the shape of every wiggle, but the number of wiggles stays put.

That's why a singer can sing a whole song, high and low, and still sound like themselves: the pitch moves, the colour travels along with it.

The big idea: pitch is how often the wave repeats; tone colour is the shape that repeats. Two knobs, independent.
5

Vowels are tone colour too

Try this out loud: sing “ahh”, then, on the very same note, change to “eee”. Your pitch didn't move, but the sound changed completely. That change is tone colour, and you make it with your mouth.

Your throat makes a buzz that is full of harmonics. Your mouth and tongue then act like a filter: they make some harmonics louder and hush others. Move your tongue and you change which ones get the boost. Each vowel is simply a different boost pattern. (Speech scientists call these boosted zones formants.)

The bottom picture shows the trick. The ladder of harmonics never moves, because the pitch never moves. Only the humps slide around, deciding which rungs of the ladder get loud. “Ee” boosts a high zone and sounds bright; “oo” keeps everything low and sounds dark, a lot like the presets in the mixer.

The big idea: every vowel is a tone colour: the same buzz at the same pitch, with different harmonics boosted by the shape of your mouth.
6

Your ear already knows

Most people can't name a note just by hearing it. (Most musicians can't either.) But almost anyone can tell a trumpet from a flute in a heartbeat, or recognise a friend from a single “hello” on the phone. Your ear is a tone-colour expert, and you have been training it your whole life.

Let's prove it. Each round plays a little phrase. Which instrument is it? It starts with three choices and the same note every time. Get a few right and it adds a fourth choice, then starts changing the notes.

The big idea: you can recognise a tone colour even when you could never name the note; timbre is one of the first things your ear notices.
