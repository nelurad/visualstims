What is a sound?air, wobbling, all the way to your ear

Before notes, chords or songs, there's the raw stuff: something shakes, and the air carries the shake to you. This page shows how, with sound on every step. Turn your volume on.

You should hear a few soft notes. Nothing? Turn your volume up. On an iPhone, also flip off the silent switch. Then tap the dark area to make ripples.
1

Sound is something wobbling

Every sound you have ever heard started with something wobbling back and forth: a guitar string, a drum skin, two tiny flaps in your throat, the cone in a speaker. The grown-up word for a wobble is a vibration.

The wobbling thing shoves the air right next to it. That air shoves the air next to it, and so on, like a push passed along a crowd. The air itself hardly goes anywhere. Only the push travels. When it reaches you it nudges your eardrum, a thin skin inside your ear, and your brain says: sound!

TRY IT · tap anywhere to pluck. Drag across the string to strum it. Watch the dots of air bunch up and spread out on their way to the ear.

The dots are air. They jiggle a little all the time. Give them something to carry.

The bunched-up bands are air squeezed a bit tighter; the gaps are air stretched a bit thinner. So a sound is really a train of squeezes and stretches, rolling outward. It's quick, too: a sound crosses a big room in about a thirtieth of a second.

The big idea: a sound is a wobble, passed through the air from the thing that shakes to the little drum inside your ear.
2

Fast wobbles sound high

Some things wobble slowly and some wobble fast, and your ear can tell. You hear the speed as pitch: how high or low a sound is.

Slow wobbles sound low, like a rumble. Fast wobbles sound high, like a squeak. That's the whole trick.

We count wobbles per second, and the unit has a name: hertz, written Hz. 100 Hz just means 100 wobbles every second. (It's named after a scientist, Heinrich Hertz. It's pronounced like "hurts", but it doesn't.)

TRY IT · press and hold the box, then slide left and right. Arrow keys work too. The picture shows one fiftieth of a second of the wobble.

Look at the picture as you slide right: the wiggles crowd closer together. More wobbles get squeezed into the same tiny slice of time, and the sound climbs.

The big idea: pitch is wobble speed. More wobbles per second (more hertz) sounds higher.
3

Big things sound low, small things sound high

Here's a rule you already know without knowing you know it. Big, heavy things wobble slowly, so they sound low. Small, light things wobble quickly, so they sound high. A church bell goes bonnng; a bicycle bell goes ting.

TRY IT · tap the bells and drums (several fingers at once is fine; keys 1 to 8 work too). Watch how fast each one shivers.

Your voice follows the same rule. Grown-ups usually have longer, heavier vocal cords (those two flaps in your throat) than kids do, so their voices sit lower. It's the same story with a cello and a violin: the big one plays the low notes.

The big idea: bigger and heavier means slower wobbles, and slower wobbles mean lower sounds.
4

Bigger wobbles sound louder

A wobble has two separate things you can change. One is how fast it goes back and forth: that's pitch. The other is how far it swings: a tiny tremble or a big, wide swing. The size of the swing is loudness.

Pluck a string gently and it barely moves; pluck it hard and it swings wide. You hear the same note, just louder. Speed and size don't depend on each other, so you can have a quiet high sound, a loud low one, or any mix.

How loud is loud?Loudness is often measured in decibels (dB). A whisper is about 30 dB, chatting is about 60, a rock concert is over 100. Very loud sound for a long time can damage your ears for good, so keep your volume comfy.
The big idea: how far it swings is loudness; how fast it swings is pitch. They are two separate knobs.
5

Steady notes and noisy noise

Hum a tune, then say "shhhh". Both are sounds, but they're different kinds. A hum is a steady wobble: the same little shape, repeating over and over, regular as a clock. Your ear locks on to the repeat and hears a definite pitch you could sing back. That's a note.

"Shhhh" is a jumble of random wobbles, fast and slow all mixed up, never repeating. There's no single pitch to grab, so you hear a hiss. That's noise.

Musical notes are the steady kind. Most drums lean noisy, which is why you can't hum the note of a snare drum. They still make music, though, by marking out time (that's lesson 5).

The big idea: a steady, repeating wobble gives a note with a pitch; a random wobble gives noise.
6

How high can you hear?

Human ears catch wobbles from about 20 Hz (a deep rumble you feel more than hear) up to about 20,000 Hz (a whisper-thin whine). Outside that range the air still wobbles, but we can't hear it. Elephants rumble to each other below it; dogs and bats hear far above it.

The top end slowly shrinks as we get older, so a teenager can hear tones their parents can't. Try it: start a sweep, and tap the button the moment the sound vanishes.

Ears firstKeep your volume at a normal level. When the tone fades away, that is the test working, so don't turn it up to chase it. Phone and laptop speakers can't play very low sounds, so the low sweep may go quiet early; headphones do better.

The big idea: we hear roughly 20 to 20,000 wobbles per second, and nearly all music lives in the comfy middle.
