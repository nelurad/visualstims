1

Notes, all at once

So far we've mostly played notes one after another, like words in a sentence. A chord is what you get when you play three or more notes at the same time. Instead of a line, you get a stack.

Listen to what happens as the stack grows. One note is plain. Two is richer. Three, and it starts to have a mood.

TRY IT · tap any white key. It becomes the bottom stone, and we stack two more on top, skipping a key each time.

Here's the trick: play a key, skip a key, play a key, skip a key, play a key.

That's the skip-a-key trick. On the white keys, C (skip D) E (skip F) G gives you the most famous chord there is: C major. The bottom note is called the root. It's the chord's name tag: this stack is "C something" because it sits on C.

Try a few different white keys. Some stacks sound sunny, some sound a bit glum, and one (starting on B) sounds like a haunted house. Why? Next chapter.

The big idea: a chord is three or more notes played at once, and "play one, skip one" on the white keys builds one instantly.
2

One tiny step from happy to sad

Every basic chord has three parts. The root at the bottom, a 3rd in the middle, and a 5th on top. (Remember intervals? A 5th is 7 half steps above the root.)

The middle note decides the mood. Put it 4 half steps above the root (a major 3rd) and you get a major chord: bright, happy. Slide it down just one half step to 3 above the root (a minor 3rd) and you get a minor chord: darker, sadder. Same root, same top. One note, one tiny step.

That's the whole secret. Musicians write a major chord as just its letter, like C, and a minor chord with a little m, like Cm (say "C minor").

The big idea: root + 3rd + 5th makes a chord, and moving the middle note down one half step turns major (happy) into minor (sad).

PLAY IT AS · the same notes, three ways

Three ways to play the same chord:

Block: all the notes hit together, like a piano player planting both hands.
Arpeggio (say "ar-PEH-jee-oh"): one note at a time, bottom to top, like a harp.
Strum: almost together, but each string a hair later than the last, the way a guitar pick drags across. The tiny delays are what make it sound like a guitar.

Look at the clock on the right. Every major chord draws the same triangle, just turned to a new spot. Every minor chord draws its mirror image. The shape is the sound.

The spice rackDiminished (°): squash the top note down too. Spooky, tense, horror-movie. Sus ("suspended"): swap the middle note for its neighbour. Floating, undecided. 7th: add a fourth note on top. Bluesy, jazzy, restless.
The big idea: the same recipe builds a chord on any of the 12 notes, and you can play it all at once, one by one, or strummed.
4

Chords come in families

Remember the C major scale, all white keys from C to C? Use the skip-a-key trick starting on each of its seven notes and you get seven chords that belong together. Musicians call this family a key. These are the chords "in the key of C".

Instead of names, musicians often just number them: chord 1, chord 4, chord 5. They use Roman numerals (I, II, III, IV, V...), with a twist: BIG numerals mean major, small ones mean minor, and a little circle means diminished.

TAP · each card is one chord from the family. Keys 1 to 7 work too.

Why numbers? Because the pattern is the same in every key. In the key of G, chord 1 is G, chord 4 is C, chord 5 is D. Say "one, four, five" to any musician and they'll know exactly what to play, whatever key the singer needs.

The big idea: stacking on each note of a scale gives seven related chords, and we call them by number: 1, 4 and 5 are the big three.
5

Home, and away from home

Chords in a key aren't all equal. Chord I is home: when a song lands on it, it feels finished, like a full stop. Chord V is the opposite. It feels like standing at the front door with your coat on. It wants to go home.

Hear it for yourself. The second button stops before going home. Notice how your brain fills in the missing chord?

That pull is what makes music feel like it's going somewhere. Songs leave home, wander, build up on V, and then (usually) come home. The tug is strongest when V gets a 7th added on top.

The big idea: chord I sounds like home and chord V leans towards it, and that pull is what makes music feel like a journey.

Four chords, a thousand songs

Here's a slightly embarrassing secret of pop music. An enormous number of hits, from the 80s to last week, use the same four chords going round and round: I – V – vi – IV. In C, that's C, G, Am, F. Home, away, a sad bit, a warm bit, repeat.

Other loops worth knowing. I – vi – IV – V is the 1950s "doo-wop" loop: think slow-dancing at a school prom. Start the pop loop on the sad chord instead, vi – IV – I – V, and it suddenly sounds moody and emotional. And the 12-bar blues isn't a four-chord loop at all: twelve bars using just I, IV and V (with a 7th on each), the backbone of blues and early rock and roll.

The big idea: a short loop of chords (most famously I – V – vi – IV) is the backbone of countless songs, and the pentatonic scale lets anyone sing or play on top.
