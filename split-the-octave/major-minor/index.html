Same tune, two moods

Here's a tune you already know. Press play, then flip the switch while it's going. The rhythm stays the same, the speed stays the same, and it starts on the same note. Only a few notes change, and the whole feeling changes with them.

Musicians call the bright version major and the darker one minor. Lots of listeners hear major as sunny or settled, and minor as moody, wistful or a bit tense. (Is that always true? Hold that thought for chapter 7.)

The dots in the picture are the notes. Watch for the ones with a little dashed tail when you're in minor: each one has dropped by a single half step from where it was in major.

The big idea: major and minor are two moods for the same tune, and the difference is only a few notes.
2

A scale is a ladder

Remember the 12 notes in an octave? Most songs don't use all 12. They pick 7 and mostly stick to those. That set of 7 is called a scale: a ladder you climb from a starting note up to the same note an octave higher.

The easiest one to find: start on C and play only the white keys. That's the C major scale: C D E F G A B, and C again at the top.

Here's the secret. The rungs are not evenly spaced. Most gaps are a whole step (W, two half steps), but two of them are half steps (H, just one). Below, the ladder is drawn as stairs: a long tread is a whole step, a short tread is a half step.

TAP a stair to hear it · tap a W or H block to hear that step · or play the whole ladder

C major, drawn as stairs

Tip: on a computer keyboard, the keys A W S E D F T G Y H U J K play the piano.

Read the pattern off the stairs: W W H W W W H. Notice where the two short treads sit: E to F and B to C. Those are exactly the two places on a piano where there's no black key squeezed between the white ones.

The big idea: a major scale is 7 notes picked by one step pattern: W W H W W W H.
3

Start anywhere, keep the pattern

The white keys aren't the real recipe. The pattern is. Start on any of the 12 notes, follow W W H W W W H, and you get a major scale that sounds just as bright, only higher or lower.

Try G. The pattern walks up the white keys happily until near the top, where it asks for a whole step up from E. But E to F is only a half step, so the recipe reaches one key further, to the black key F♯. That's why G major has an F♯ in it. The black keys aren't extras; they're the notes the pattern needs.

PICK a starting note · watch which black keys get pulled in

The note a scale starts on is its home note. When people say a song is "in G major", they mean it lives on the G major ladder and feels most at rest when it lands on G.

The big idea: every major scale uses the same step pattern; the starting note decides which black keys join in.
4

The shady ladder

Minor has its own pattern: W H W W H W W. Same number of rungs, same height top to bottom, but the short steps sit in different places. This one is called the natural minor scale (it has a couple of cousins you'll meet another day).

C major W W H W W W H

C minor W H W W H W W

Compare them rung by rung. Three notes sit one half step lower in minor: the 3rd, the 6th and the 7th (the dashed lines show where they used to be). The one that matters most is the 3rd. From C up to E is 4 half steps: a major 3rd, from the intervals lesson. From C up to E♭ is only 3: a minor 3rd. That one small drop carries most of the mood.

PLAY the loop · then lower the 3rd (and only the 3rd) while it plays

The big idea: the minor pattern is W H W W H W W, and its lowered 3rd is the note your ear notices first.
5

A happy chord, a sad chord

Take three rungs from the ladder, the 1st, the 3rd and the 5th, and play them at the same time. That's a chord: notes sounding together. Use the major ladder and you get a major chord. Drop the 3rd by a half step and it becomes a minor chord.

Look at the gaps. A major chord is 4 half steps, then 3. A minor chord is 3, then 4. Same two pieces, swapped round. That swap is the entire difference.

FLIP between major and minor · tap a dot to hear one note · pick another root

Chords get a whole lesson next. For now, a handy rule: when a single chord on its own sounds sad or serious, it's almost always a minor chord.

The big idea: root + 3rd + 5th makes a chord; move the 3rd down one half step and major turns minor.
6

Same keys, different home

Here's a twist. Play only the white keys again, but start and finish on A instead of C. Count the steps: W H W W H W W. That's the minor pattern! A minor and C major use exactly the same seven notes.

So what makes one of them sound minor? Where the music comes to rest. A melody that keeps settling on C, with C in the bass, sounds major. Move the resting place to A and the very same notes sound minor. Musicians call A minor the relative minor of C major: same family of notes, different home.

The big idea: major or minor is about which note feels like home, not only which notes you use.
7

Habits, not laws

Be a little suspicious of "major = happy, minor = sad". It's a strong habit in Western music (most of what you hear on the radio), and we soak it up as kids. But speed, rhythm, loudness, the instruments and the words push the mood around too, sometimes harder than the scale does.

minor, but you'll dance
  • "Billie Jean", Michael Jackson
  • "Smooth Criminal", Michael Jackson
  • lots of folk dances and wedding tunes
major, but have a tissue
  • "Someone Like You", Adele
  • "Tears in Heaven", Eric Clapton

The party tune is the minor Twinkle from chapter 1, played fast with drums. The sigh is in a major key, played slow and soft and falling. (Sad songs in major keys often borrow a minor chord or two along the way, but their home is still major.)

The big idea: major and minor nudge the mood, but speed, rhythm and context can push it almost anywhere.
