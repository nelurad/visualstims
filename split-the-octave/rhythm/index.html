Beat & rhythmthe heartbeat under every song

So far we've looked at pitch: high notes and low notes. Now for the other half of music: time. When sounds happen, how long they last, and the steady pulse that makes you nod your head. By the end you'll build your own drum beat. Turn your volume on.

1

The beat: music's heartbeat

Put on almost any song and your foot starts tapping on its own. That steady tap, tap, tap is the beat (people also call it the pulse). It's like a heartbeat or a ticking clock: evenly spaced, and it keeps going even when the music goes quiet.

Start the beat and watch the ball. It lands exactly on every beat, and its footprints come out evenly spaced. That's what "steady" looks like.

2

Tempo: how fast the beat goes

The speed of the beat is the tempo (Italian for "time"). We measure it in beats per minute, or BPM: how many taps fit into one minute. 60 BPM is one beat every second, like a clock ticking. 120 BPM is two beats a second.

Turn the dial. The same little tune plays faster or slower, and the walker keeps in step.

TRY IT · drag around the dial (or use the arrow keys), press play, and keep turning

Notice what doesn't change: the tune. Same notes, same pattern, only squeezed or stretched in time. Slow tempos tend to feel calm, sleepy or sad. Fast ones feel excited, even frantic.

The big idea: tempo is the speed of the beat, counted in beats per minute; low numbers feel calm and high numbers feel urgent.
3

Strong and weak: counting in groups

Beats aren't all equal. Some lean harder than others, and your brain sorts them into little groups without asking. Clap along to a pop song and you'll find yourself counting ONE two three four, ONE two three four. That heavy ONE is the strong beat.

Each group is called a bar (or a measure; same thing). In written music a little upright line, the bar line, marks where one bar ends and the next begins.

Most pop, rock and marching music counts in 4. A waltz counts in 3: ONE two three, ONE two three, which makes you want to sway or spin. Switch between them and listen for where the heavy ONE lands.

TRY IT · press play, switch between 4 and 3, then try flattening the accents

With the accents flattened, every beat is the same and it gets surprisingly hard to tell 3 from 4. The strong beat is the clue your ear uses to find the groups.

The big idea: beats come in groups called bars, and the strong ONE at the start of each bar tells you whether you're counting in 4 (march) or 3 (waltz).
4

Long notes and short notes

The beat is the steady floor. Rhythm is what dances on top of it: the pattern of long and short sounds.

Think of one bar of 4 beats as a chocolate bar. You can eat it in one big piece, snap it in half, into quarters or into eighths. Musicians have a name for each size of piece. Click a row to hear it over a soft ticking beat.

TRY IT · click any row · the ticks are the beat, the flute is the rhythm

A whole note fills the whole bar (4 beats). A half note lasts 2 beats, a quarter note 1 beat, and an eighth note half a beat, so two eighths fit into one tap of your foot. Every row is the very same bar, just cut up differently. The little symbols are how each one is written in sheet music.

You already know lots of rhythms by heart. Here are a few drawn as boxes: a wider box is a longer sound, a dashed one is silence. Press one and watch it light up.

The big idea: rhythm is the pattern of long and short notes laid over the beat; a whole note fills the bar, and each smaller note lasts half as long as the one before.
5

Rests: silence counts too

A gap in the sound isn't a mistake. It's a rest: a silence with a set length, and it's part of the rhythm. The beat keeps ticking underneath even when nobody plays. Rests let the music breathe, and they make the next note hit harder.

6

Syncopation: landing between the beats

When the notes land right on the beat, music feels solid and square, like marching. Count "one and two and three and four and". When notes land on those "ands", in between the beats, the music suddenly feels bouncy and a little surprising. Leaning on the off-beats like this is called syncopation, and it's the secret sauce of funk, reggae, Latin music and most pop.

Below, the drums never change. Only the keyboard stabs move. Switch while it plays and feel your body react.

TRY IT · press play, then switch between the three patterns

The "pushed" pattern splits the bar's eight half-beats into 3 + 3 + 2. It turns up all over the world, from Cuban music (where it's called tresillo) to today's pop and reggaeton. You'll meet it again in a minute.

The big idea: syncopation means putting accents between the beats instead of on them, and that little surprise is what makes music feel funky.
7

Build a beat

Time to put it all together. This is a step sequencer, the grid that producers use to make drum beats. One bar is chopped into 16 little steps, four for each beat. Each row is a drum. Light up a box and that drum hits on that step.

The kick is the deep thump, the snare the crack, the hi-hat the "tss". Start from a preset and change it: drag across boxes to paint, tap to erase. Then try swing, which nudges every other step a tiny bit late and turns stiff into groovy.

The big idea: a drum beat is just the ideas from this page stacked up: a steady pulse, strong and weak beats, short notes, rests, and a few hits pushed off the beat.
