Split the Octavemusic's twelve notes, from scratch

Why does a piano have 12 notes that repeat? Why do some notes sound good together? This page starts from nothing (what a sound even is) and builds up to scales and chords. Every idea has sound, so turn your volume on.

You should hear a few soft notes. Nothing? Turn your volume up. On an iPhone, also flip off the silent switch.
1

Sound is air, wobbling

Everything you hear is air being pushed back and forth. A guitar string, your vocal cords, a speaker: each one shoves the air next to it many times a second, and those ripples travel to your eardrum.

How many times per second the air wobbles is the frequency, measured in hertz (Hz). 440 Hz means 440 wobbles every second. Your brain hears frequency as pitch: slow wobbles sound low, fast wobbles sound high.

Young people can hear roughly 20 to 20,000 Hz. Almost all music lives between about 30 and 4,000 Hz.

Inside your earDeep in your inner ear is the cochlea, a coiled tube with a membrane running along it. High frequencies shake the membrane near the entrance (the base). Low frequencies travel all the way to the far tip (the apex). Each spot has its own hair cells and nerve fibres, so which spot shakes tells your brain the pitch. 2

Double it, and it's the same note

Here is the strangest fact in music. Play 220 Hz, then 440, then 880. Each one wobbles twice as fast as the one before. They clearly go up, and yet they sound like the same note, just higher.

Doubling the frequency is called going up an octave. Notes an octave apart get the same name: 220, 440 and 880 are all called A. 660 sounds like a different note (an E).

So pitch has two parts. Height: how high or low. And chroma, the "note-ness": which named note it is. You can picture pitch as a spiral staircase. Every full turn is one octave, and notes stacked directly above each other share a name.

The pitch spiral. Play the buttons above and watch where the notes land: same direction, different floors.

From here on, each colour is one note name. All the A's are the same colour, at every height.

Why would doubling sound "the same"?When one wave wobbles exactly twice as fast as another, every second wobble of the fast one lines up with a wobble of the slow one. They fit together so well that your brain blends them into one sound (try "together" above). Babies treat octaves as alike, and so do rhesus monkeys, so this is about how hearing works, not just something we learn from music.

Your ear hears times, not plus

Try this. Both pairs below go up by exactly 110 Hz. Listen to how different they sound.

The first is a huge leap: a whole octave. The second is barely a step. Now both pairs below double the frequency:

They sound like the same size jump. Your ear doesn't count hertz. It hears how many times bigger one frequency is than another: a ratio. ×2 always sounds like an octave, whether it's 110→220 or 880→1760.

4

Why some notes sound good together

A real instrument never plays just one frequency. A string vibrating at 110 Hz also vibrates in halves (220 Hz), thirds (330), quarters (440), and so on. These extra frequencies are called harmonics (or overtones). The mix of harmonics is why a piano and a flute sound different even on the same note.

A note at 110 Hz built from 8 harmonics. Press play, then click the bars to switch harmonics on and off.

Notice that you hear one note, not eight. Your brain glues the harmonics together. Now press remove the 1st. The 110 Hz harmonic is gone, yet the pitch stays at 110. Your brain works out the missing fundamental from the pattern of the others. That's why you can still hear a bass line on tiny phone speakers that can't play low notes at all.

Now play two notes at once. Their harmonics overlap. If the two frequencies make a simple ratio, many harmonics land exactly on top of each other and the sound is smooth. If they're slightly off, nearby harmonics clash, and you hear a throbbing called beating as the waves slide in and out of step.

Two notes. The lower is fixed at 220 Hz. Press play and slowly drag the upper note toward 3:2. Listen to the throbbing slow down, then disappear.

lowerhigher

That smooth spot, exactly 3:2, is called a perfect fifth. It's the smoothest pair after the octave. In general, the simpler the ratio, the smoother two notes sound together: 2:1 (octave), 3:2 (fifth), 4:3 (fourth), 5:4 (major third). This is the raw material of harmony.

5

The circle that won't close

The fifth (3:2) sounds so good that ancient tuners, starting with the followers of Pythagoras, built every note out of it. Start on C. Go up a fifth (×1.5). Go up another fifth. Keep going. Whenever you climb past the octave, drop down an octave (÷2) to stay in range.

After 12 fifths you've visited 12 different notes, and you should land back on C. Do you?

The spiral shows each note's place in the octave. It gets further from the centre each step so you can see the path.

You land slightly too high. Twelve fifths multiply the frequency by (3/2)12 = 129.75. Seven octaves multiply it by 27 = 128. They don't match. The leftover, about 1.4%, is called the Pythagorean comma. Play the start and end together and you'll hear them beat against each other.

The catch: pure ratios can never fill an octave evenly. Tune a piano with pure fifths and one fifth ends up badly out of tune. Old tuners called it the wolf because it howled.

6

The fix: twelve equal steps

12-tone equal temperament (12-TET) stops chasing perfect ratios. It cuts the octave into 12 steps that are all exactly the same size.

Remember, the ear hears ratios. So "the same size" means every step multiplies the frequency by the same number. Twelve steps have to make one octave (×2), so the step is the number that gives 2 when you multiply it by itself 12 times. That's the 12th root of 2:

one step = 12√2 ≈ 1.05946   →   1.05946 × 1.05946 × … (12 times) = 2Each step makes the frequency about 5.9% higher. This step is called a semitone (or half step).

So any note is just a starting note times that step, over and over. With A = 440 Hz as the anchor:

frequency = 440 × 2n/12   (n = semitones away from A)n = 12 → 880 Hz (an octave up). n = 7 → 659.26 Hz (a fifth up; a pure 3:2 would be 660).

Musicians measure these steps in cents: 100 cents per semitone, 1200 per octave. Cents follow the ear's ruler, so 100 cents always sounds like the same size step, anywhere on the piano.

Why 12, and not 10 or 17? Change the number of steps below. The gold line is a pure 3:2 fifth. The coloured line is the closest step the system has to it.

The bars show how far each system's best fifth misses a pure one. 12 is the smallest number of steps that nails the fifth: only 2 cents flat, below what most people can hear. (Trained listeners can tell apart notes about 5–10 cents apart.) 19, 24, 31 and 53 also do well, but every extra step means more keys, frets and strings.

7

Meet the twelve notes

Those 12 equal steps are the 12 keys of a piano, repeating every octave. The white keys get letter names A B C D E F G. The black keys sit in between and borrow a neighbour's name: ♯ (sharp) means one semitone up, ♭ (flat) one semitone down. So the black key between C and D is both C♯ and D♭: one key, two names.

Click the keys, or play on your computer keyboard: A W S E D F T G Y H U J K O L P.

Play a key to see its frequency.

Look for the two places with no black key: E–F and B–C. Those white keys are only one semitone apart. Everywhere else, white neighbours are two semitones apart (a whole step).

The wheel on the right is the same 12 notes as a clock. Going once around is one octave, because after B comes C again.

8

The twelve intervals

An interval is the distance between two notes, counted in semitones. Inside one octave there are exactly 12, and each has its own feel. Click one to hear it one note after the other (melody), then both together (harmony).

Why "third" for 3 or 4 semitones? The names count letters, not semitones. C up to E touches C, D, E: three letters, so it's a third. "Major" and "minor" are the bigger and smaller versions. "Perfect" goes on the fourth, fifth and octave, the intervals with the simplest ratios.

The pattern to notice: intervals near simple ratios (octave, fifth, fourth) sound stable. Their neighbours one semitone away (the minor second, major seventh, tritone) sound tense, as if they want to move somewhere.

9

The price of equal

12-TET gets the fifth almost perfect (2 cents off). It's less lucky with thirds. A pure major third is 5:4 = 386 cents. The piano's is 400 cents, 14 cents too wide. Listen to a C major chord (C, E, G) tuned both ways:

The pure chord sounds still and glassy. The equal one shimmers slightly, because the third is beating. Choirs, string quartets and barbershop singers slide toward pure thirds without thinking about it. Pianos and guitars can't, because their notes are fixed.

That's the deal 12-TET makes: every chord is a little bit off, so that every key works equally well. The next chapter shows why that's worth it.

Scales: a pattern of steps

A scale picks some of the 12 notes to make melodies from. The major scale ("do re mi") is a pattern of whole steps (W = 2 semitones) and half steps (H = 1):

Pick a starting note (the key) and a scale. Then play it, or play a tune in it.

Change the starting note. The scale uses different keys, but it sounds like the same scale, just higher or lower. On the wheel, the shape simply rotates. This is transposition, and it's the whole point of equal temperament. Because every semitone is identical, a pattern of steps sounds the same no matter where it starts.

Then try Twinkle in minor. Only three notes change (the 3rd, 6th and 7th move down a semitone), and the same tune turns sad.

11

Chords: stacking thirds

A chord is three or more notes played together. The basic chord, a triad, stacks two thirds on top of each other:

The triangle on the wheel is the chord's shape. A major chord always has the same triangle, and a minor chord is its mirror image. I–V–vi–IV means: the chords built on the 1st, 5th, 6th and 4th notes of the scale (upper-case for major, lower-case for minor). That loop of four chords is behind thousands of pop songs.

12

Between the keys

12 isn't the only choice, just the most common one in Western music. Arabic, Persian and Turkish music use pitches that fall between the piano keys. One way to describe them is to split every semitone in half: 24-TET, with quarter tones of 50 cents.

The middle third (350 cents) is neither major nor minor. It's called a neutral third. It may sound "out of tune" at first only because your ears were trained on 12 notes.
