Naming the notesseven letters, round and round

Music doesn’t use every pitch. It picks out certain ones and gives them names: just seven letters, used over and over again. By the end of this page you’ll find any white key on a piano and play a tune with it. Turn your volume on.

You should hear a few soft notes. Nothing? Turn your volume up. On an iPhone, also flip off the silent switch.
1

Stairs, not a ramp

In lesson 1 you heard that pitch is how high or low a sound is. A siren slides smoothly through every pitch on the way up, like rolling up a ramp.

Music mostly doesn’t do that. It hops between a set of chosen pitches, like walking up stairs. Each stair is a note: one particular pitch with a name.

TRY IT · press and drag up and down on the pad. Then flip on “snap to notes” and drag again.

Silent. Press on the pad to make a sound.

Hear the difference? The slide is smeary and wailing. The stairs sound like music, even when you’re just dragging a finger. Every song you know is built out of these steps.

The big idea: music picks out certain pitches, called notes, and steps between them like stairs.
2

Seven letters, then round again

The notes are named with letters of the alphabet, but only the first seven: A B C D E F G. After G, there is no H. You go back to A and start again.

So the names go round in a loop: …E F G A B C D E F G A B… like the days of the week. After Sunday comes Monday again, not “day eight”.

TRY IT · tap the stepping stones, or let it walk. Watch the little loop of letters go round.

Each time the letters come round, the notes keep getting higher. The A at the end sounds like the A at the start, only higher. That’s not a coincidence. It’s the whole next chapter.

Wait, what about the black keys?Pianos also have black keys squeezed in between. They’re real notes too, and they get their names in the next lesson. Today is all about the seven letters.
The big idea: notes are named A, B, C, D, E, F, G, and then the letters start over.
3

The same note, only higher

Why do the letters repeat? Because your ear says they should. Play a C, then the next C up. They sound weirdly alike: the same note, just in a higher place. Now play C then D. Those sound like two different notes.

TRY IT · pick a pair, then listen one after the other, and together.

Here is the secret. The higher C wobbles exactly twice as fast as the lower one. Every wobble of the low C fits two wobbles of the high one, so they line up again and again (see the bright lines). C and D hardly ever line up.

That jump, from a note to the one with twice the wobbles, is called an octave. Notes an octave apart get the same letter. When men and women sing the “same” tune together, they’re usually an octave apart without even trying.

The big idea: double the wobbles and you get the same note, higher. That’s an octave, and it’s why the letters repeat.
4

Finding your way on a piano

A piano is just the stairs laid out flat. Each key is one note. The white keys are the seven letters, over and over. But they all look the same, so how do you know which is which?

Look at the black keys. They come in little groups: two, then three, then two, then three, all the way along. Call them the twins and the triplets. They’re your landmarks.

C is the white key just left of the twins.
F is the white key just left of the triplets.

5

Low on the left, high on the right

On a piano, pitch goes sideways. The far left is low and growly; the far right is high and tinkly. Each step to the right is one stair up.

Since there are lots of Cs, we give each one a number to say which C. Think of the keyboard as a building with floors. Each floor starts on a C and runs up to B. Count floors from the bottom of a full-size piano, and the C nearest the middle lands on floor 4. So it’s called C4, or middle C. The E just above it is E4; the next C up is C5.

6

A tune is a walk up and down

Now you can read a tune without any special symbols. Here a melody is drawn as dots: higher dots are higher notes, and left to right is the order you play them. A tune is just a little walk up and down the stairs.

TRY IT · pick a song and press play. Tap any dot to hear it. Then try “your turn” and follow the glowing key.

Twinkle Twinkle uses just six notes: C D E F G A.

Notice the shape. Twinkle jumps up, floats a bit, then walks back down one step at a time. Mary wobbles around three notes like someone pacing. You can hear the shape before you know a single note name.

The big idea: a melody is a path of notes going up and down, and you can draw it as dots.
7

A sneak peek at sheet music

Sheet music is the same dot idea, tidied up. The dots sit on a ladder of five lines called a staff. Each line and each gap between lines is one white key. Higher on the page means higher in pitch. That’s honestly most of it. We’ll learn to read it properly much later.

TRY IT · tap or drag on the lines to write a tune. Each tap adds the next note. Then play it back.

Notes higher on the staff are higher notes.
The big idea: on a staff, higher on the page means a higher note, one white key per line or gap.
